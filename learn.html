The capacitated location-routing problem

A company must decide where to open depots and how to route vehicles from those depots to customers. The two decisions depend on each other: a cheap depot far from its customers makes expensive routes, and a central depot may cost too much to open. The capacitated location-routing problem (CLRP) asks for the cheapest combination.

Objective

Minimise the total of three costs:

  1. Depot opening costs of the depots that are used,
  2. Fixed vehicle costs, one amount for every route (vehicle) used, and
  3. Routing costs, the length of all routes.

Constraints

  • Single assignment. Every customer is visited exactly once, by one route, and its whole demand is delivered.
  • Vehicle capacity. The demands on one route add up to at most the vehicle capacity Q.
  • Depot capacity. The demands on all routes of one depot add up to at most the capacity of that depot, and only open depots can serve routes.
  • Route–depot consistency. A route starts and ends at the same depot.

Mathematical model

The problem is written below as a set-partitioning model over routes, the form on which the exact method of is built. The heuristics on this site never build this model: they search over routes directly.

Sets
Icandidate depots, indexed by i
Jcustomers, indexed by j
Ωifeasible routes of depot i, defined below
Data
fiopening cost of depot i
Wicapacity of depot i
qjdemand of customer j
Qcapacity of one vehicle
Ffixed cost of one vehicle, paid once for every route
duvdistance between two nodes u, v ∈ I ∪ J
Decision variables
xr1 if route r is driven, 0 otherwise
yi1 if depot i is open, 0 otherwise

A route r ∈ Ωi of depot i is a sequence (i, j1, …, jk, i) of k ≥ 1 distinct customers. Its demand must not exceed Q, and its length is the total distance driven:

qr = k∑t = 1 qjt   ≤   Q
cr = di j1 + k − 1∑t = 1 djt jt+1 + djk i

The indicator ajr equals 1 if route r visits customer j and 0 otherwise.

min ∑i ∈ I fi yi + ∑i ∈ I ∑r ∈ Ωi (F + cr) xr(1)
∑i ∈ I ∑r ∈ Ωi ajr xr = 1   ∀ j ∈ J(2)
∑r ∈ Ωi qr xr ≤ Wi yi   ∀ i ∈ I(3)
xr ∈ {0, 1}   ∀ i ∈ I, r ∈ Ωi(4)
yi ∈ {0, 1}   ∀ i ∈ I(5)
  • (1) minimises the total cost: opening costs of the open depots, one vehicle cost for every route, and the total route length.
  • (2) every customer is visited by exactly one route.
  • (3) the demands on all routes of a depot add up to at most its capacity, and a closed depot (yi = 0) serves no route.
  • (4), (5) binary variables. The vehicle capacity Q is part of the definition of Ωi. The number of vehicles and the number of open depots are not limited.

The set Ωi grows exponentially with the number of customers, so the model is not solved directly here.

Distances are Euclidean. In the P instances they are multiplied by 100 and rounded up to whole numbers, as stated by and as in the code and data of ; the B and T instances use real distances (see file formats). The solver does not limit the number of vehicles or depots: both come out of the optimisation. The problem is NP-hard, so large instances are solved by heuristics. Instances and best known values

About the best known solution (BKS)

The BKS of an instance is the lowest cost we took from the published tables of the literature (mainly , together with the tables of , and ). These values are published snapshots, not certified optima and not guaranteed to be the current global best: a run can legitimately end with a negative gap. Note on the P instances: the creators of the set state that distances are multiplied by 100 and rounded up (; repeated by ), which is what this site and the HGAMP code do. One recent preprint says “nearest integer” instead; we follow the creators of the set. The gap on the Solver page is gap = 100 × z − zBKSzBKS %, where z is the cost of the solution.

File formats for your own instances

The Solver reads two kinds of files.

Benchmark .dat layout. Blocks separated by blank lines. Block 1: number of customers n and number of depots m. Then either (B and P files) the depot coordinates (m lines), the customer coordinates (n lines), or (T files) all coordinates in one block, depots first. Then: vehicle capacity, depot capacities (m values), customer demands (n values), depot opening costs (m values), the fixed cost of a vehicle, and a final flag: 0 means distances are rounded up after multiplying by 100, any other value means real Euclidean distances.

Simple .json layout. Distances are rounded up after multiplying by 100. Download the example from the Solver page and edit it:

{ "vehicle_capacity": 40, "vehicle_costs": 20, "depots": [ { "x": 10, "y": 10, "capacity": 70, "costs": 100 }, ... ], "customers": [ { "x": 20, "y": 15, "demand": 12 }, ... ] }

Uploads are processed in your browser and are never sent anywhere. Files are limited to 5 MB, 500 customers and 50 depots.

Local search

A local search improves a solution by repeating small changes (moves) as long as one of them lowers the cost. A move selection rule decides how the operators are tried. Pick a rule and press Play to see the descent loop on a made-up problem with five operators; every “improvement” is invented, only the control flow is real.

Where the operators appear in the literature

The matrix compares our operators with the neighbourhoods described in CLRP papers. ✓ the paper describes this move; ≈ a similar, partial or implicit counterpart (see the notes); · not found in the sections we read. A dagger (†) marks papers we did not re-read for this site: their column rests on secondary information, so “·” there means “not checked”. The cells state what a paper contains; they do not rank the methods.

  • Prins et al. and Duhamel et al. use three neighbourhoods (MOVE, SWAP, OPT) with a first-improving rule and keep the depot set fixed during local search.
  • Löffler et al. use a VND over 13 operators in a fixed order (Relocate, Exchange, Split and 2-opt* variants) and a separate refinement of the depot configuration; their single-operator removal test is the basis of the operator list here.
  • “Reassign a route to another depot” is a separate neighbourhood only in Derbel et al. (2011); in the other papers it is implicit in their moves between routes of different depots.
  • Derbel et al. (2011) is the conference version of the VNS for the CLRP (Barreto’s instances only). Ting and Chen use insertion, swap and 2-opt inside an ant colony algorithm; their 2-opt between routes is described as in Prins et al., hence “≈”.
  • Prins et al. (2006b) use the same three moves as Prins et al. (2007). In both, a depot is opened only by the crossover or the repair, not by the local search. The OPT move between routes allows several ways of reconnecting; the paper does not list them, hence “≈”.
  • Yu et al. apply insertion, swap and a modified 2-opt to a single string that holds all routes, the depots and dummy zeros, so a move can stay inside a route or cross routes and can involve depot nodes; the cells are therefore “≈”.
  • Jarboui et al. solve a variant (one uncapacitated vehicle per depot, no vehicle capacity). The column lists their moves only; their results are not comparable with CLRP values. Their segment moves (Or-opt, inverse Or-opt) cover our two-customer moves as a special case, and their add/drop depot move closes one depot and opens another.
  • Contardo et al. cycle through seven procedures. Their “facility swap” gives all routes of an open depot to a closed one; “route swap” exchanges two routes between depots; “giant tour split” merges the routes of a depot and cuts them again optimally.
  • The reversed-tail and reversed-pair variants (_rev) are our own extensions of the basic moves.
  • Our operators scan the whole neighbourhood (no granular pruning) and accept only feasible solutions; some of the cited papers use granular neighbourhoods or allow temporary capacity violations.
